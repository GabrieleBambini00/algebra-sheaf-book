# Chapter 17: Presheaves in Precise Form

In Chapter 16 we developed an intuitive picture of presheaves: assignments of data to open sets, together with restriction maps that let us "zoom in" from a larger region to a smaller one. We drew diagrams, told stories, and built the conceptual scaffolding that makes the formal definitions feel natural rather than arbitrary. In Chapter 15, we introduced the language of category theory — objects, morphisms, functors, natural transformations — as a universal grammar for mathematical structure.

Now the time has come to merge these two streams. In this chapter, we state the precise definition of a presheaf, derive its elementary consequences, and construct the categorical machinery that will carry us, in the chapters to come, from presheaves to sheaves and beyond. Every intuition from Chapter 16 will find its formal counterpart here. Every categorical abstraction from Chapter 15 will find a concrete, geometric instantiation.

The reward for this labor of precision is substantial. Once the definitions are in place, we will be able to state the sheaf condition (Chapter 18) as a single, clean categorical statement, and we will be able to prove theorems about sheaves by invoking the general theory of functors and natural transformations rather than repeating ad hoc arguments for each new example. Formalization is not the enemy of understanding; it is the instrument that transforms understanding into power.

> **Guiding Principle.** A presheaf is a contravariant functor from the category of open sets to a category of algebraic objects. Everything else — restriction maps, compatibility conditions, morphisms of presheaves, stalks, germs — is a consequence of unpacking this single sentence.

---

## Guiding Idea of the Chapter

The intuitive picture from Chapter 16 can be summarized in three bullet points:

1. **To every open set $U$ of a topological space $X$, we assign a set (or group, or ring, or vector space) $F(U)$ of "local data" defined on $U$.**
2. **Whenever $V \subseteq U$, we have a restriction map $\operatorname{res}_{U,V}: F(U) \to F(V)$ that extracts the data on $V$ from the data on $U$.**
3. **Restriction maps compose in the obvious way: restricting from $U$ to $V$ and then from $V$ to $W$ is the same as restricting directly from $U$ to $W$.**

The task of this chapter is to recognize that these three statements are precisely the definition of a *contravariant functor* from a certain category (the poset of open sets, ordered by inclusion) to another category (sets, abelian groups, vector spaces, or rings). Once we see this, the entire theory of presheaves becomes a special case of the general theory of functors, and we inherit for free a vast body of categorical machinery.

---

## 17.1 The Category of Open Sets $\operatorname{Op}(X)$

Let $(X, \tau)$ be a topological space. We construct a category $\operatorname{Op}(X)$ as follows.

**Objects.** The objects of $\operatorname{Op}(X)$ are the open sets of $X$: every $U \in \tau$ is an object.

**Morphisms.** For any two open sets $U, V \in \tau$, the morphism set is:

$$
\operatorname{Hom}_{\operatorname{Op}(X)}(U, V) =
\begin{cases}
\{ \iota_{U,V} \} & \text{if } U \subseteq V, \\
\emptyset & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
$$

That is, there is exactly one morphism from $U$ to $V$ when $U$ is contained in $V$, and no morphism otherwise. The unique morphism $\iota_{U,V}$ represents the inclusion $U \hookrightarrow V$.

**Composition.** If $U \subseteq V$ and $V \subseteq W$, then $U \subseteq W$, and we define $\iota_{V,W} \circ \iota_{U,V} = \iota_{U,W}$. This is the only possible definition, since there is at most one morphism between any two objects.

**Identity.** For each $U$, the identity morphism is $\iota_{U,U}$, which exists because $U \subseteq U$.

> **Observation.** The category $\operatorname{Op}(X)$ is a **poset category**: a category in which every hom-set has at most one element. In a poset category, the existence of a morphism $U \to V$ is equivalent to the relation $U \subseteq V$. Composition corresponds to transitivity of the order relation, and identities correspond to reflexivity.

This is a small but important conceptual shift. Before, we thought of "$U \subseteq V$" as a *relation* between sets. Now we think of it as a *morphism* in a category. The benefit is that the entire apparatus of category theory — functors, natural transformations, limits, colimits — now applies directly to the topology of $X$.

### Example: The Real Line

Consider $X = \mathbb{R}$ with the standard topology. The objects of $\operatorname{Op}(\mathbb{R})$ include every open interval $(a, b)$, every union of open intervals, the empty set $\emptyset$, and $\mathbb{R}$ itself. The morphism $(0,1) \to (-1, 2)$ exists because $(0,1) \subseteq (-1, 2)$. The morphism $(0,1) \to (2,3)$ does not exist because $(0,1) \not\subseteq (2,3)$.

### Example: The Circle

For $X = S^1$, the open sets are open arcs and their unions. The category $\operatorname{Op}(S^1)$ encodes the combinatorics of how these arcs overlap — information that will be essential when we study the gluing condition for sheaves.

## 17.2 Presheaf as a Contravariant Functor

We now give the central definition of the chapter.

**Definition 17.1 (Presheaf).** Let $X$ be a topological space and let $\mathcal{C}$ be a category. A **presheaf on $X$ with values in $\mathcal{C}$** is a contravariant functor

$$
F: \operatorname{Op}(X)^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathcal{C}.
$$

Equivalently, $F$ is a covariant functor from the opposite category $\operatorname{Op}(X)^{\mathrm{op}}$ to $\mathcal{C}$.

Let us unpack this definition carefully. A contravariant functor $F$ consists of:

1. **An assignment on objects.** For every open set $U \subseteq X$, an object $F(U)$ in $\mathcal{C}$. We call $F(U)$ the **sections of $F$ over $U$**, or the **local data of $F$ on $U$**.

2. **An assignment on morphisms.** For every inclusion $V \subseteq U$ (i.e., every morphism $\iota_{V,U}: V \to U$ in $\operatorname{Op}(X)$), a morphism

$$
\operatorname{res}_{U,V} = F(\iota_{V,U}): F(U) \to F(V)
$$

in $\mathcal{C}$. Note the **reversal of direction**: the inclusion $V \hookrightarrow U$ goes "from small to large," but the restriction map $\operatorname{res}_{U,V}$ goes "from large to small." This is the hallmark of contravariance.

These assignments must satisfy two axioms:

**(Axiom P1: Identity.)** For every open set $U$,

$$
\operatorname{res}_{U,U} = \operatorname{id}_{F(U)}.
$$

Restricting from $U$ to itself does nothing.

**(Axiom P2: Composition.)** For every chain of inclusions $W \subseteq V \subseteq U$,

$$
\operatorname{res}_{V,W} \circ \operatorname{res}_{U,V} = \operatorname{res}_{U,W}.
$$

Restricting in two steps is the same as restricting in one step.

> **Key Insight.** The two axioms (P1) and (P2) are not ad hoc requirements. They are the *functoriality axioms* — the requirements that every functor must satisfy. By recognizing a presheaf as a functor, we see that these axioms are not special to sheaf theory; they are the universal axioms of structure preservation.

The contravariance is the conceptual heart of the definition. In topology, inclusion maps go from smaller sets to larger sets. But if we think of $F(U)$ as the "information available on $U$," then having a *larger* open set gives us *more* information, and restriction *discards* information by focusing on a smaller region. The information flows in the opposite direction to the inclusion of sets.

## 17.3 Target Categories

The definition of a presheaf is parametric in the target category $\mathcal{C}$. The choice of $\mathcal{C}$ determines the *type* of algebraic data that the presheaf assigns to each open set. Here are the most important cases.

**Presheaves of sets** ($\mathcal{C} = \mathbf{Set}$). To each open set $U$, we assign a set $F(U)$, and restriction maps are functions between sets. This is the most general setting and the one used in topos theory. No algebraic structure is assumed on the sections.

**Presheaves of abelian groups** ($\mathcal{C} = \mathbf{Ab}$). To each open set $U$, we assign an abelian group $F(U)$, and restriction maps are group homomorphisms. This is the default setting in algebraic geometry and algebraic topology. It allows us to add and subtract sections, which is essential for defining exact sequences and cohomology.

**Presheaves of rings** ($\mathcal{C} = \mathbf{Ring}$). To each open set $U$, we assign a ring $F(U)$, and restriction maps are ring homomorphisms. The structure sheaf $\mathcal{O}_X$ of a ringed space is the prototypical example: $\mathcal{O}_X(U)$ is the ring of functions (continuous, smooth, holomorphic, or algebraic, depending on the context) defined on $U$.

**Presheaves of vector spaces** ($\mathcal{C} = \mathbf{Vect}_k$). To each open set $U$, we assign a vector space $F(U)$ over a field $k$, and restriction maps are linear maps. This is the natural setting for studying vector bundles and their sections, connecting directly to the linear algebra of Chapters 1--3.

**Presheaves of modules** ($\mathcal{C} = \mathbf{Mod}_R$). To each open set $U$, we assign an $R$-module $F(U)$, and restriction maps are $R$-module homomorphisms. When $R$ is a sheaf of rings (the structure sheaf), this gives the theory of $\mathcal{O}_X$-modules, which is the algebraic backbone of modern algebraic geometry.

> **Remark.** The choice of target category is not merely a matter of generality for its own sake. Different target categories capture different kinds of local data. A presheaf of sets might record the set of solutions to an equation on each open set. A presheaf of abelian groups might record the group of differential forms. A presheaf of rings might record the ring of regular functions. The target category tells you what *kind* of mathematics you can do with the sections.

## 17.4 The Restriction Morphisms, Formally

Let us examine the restriction morphisms more carefully. Fix a presheaf $F: \operatorname{Op}(X)^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathcal{C}$ and an element $s \in F(U)$ (a "section over $U$"). For any open $V \subseteq U$, we write

$$
s|_V := \operatorname{res}_{U,V}(s) \in F(V).
$$

This notation, the vertical bar with subscript, is standard and evocative: $s|_V$ is the "restriction of $s$ to $V$," the result of observing the global datum $s$ only on the smaller region $V$.

The two presheaf axioms translate into:

- $s|_U = s$ for all $s \in F(U)$. (Restricting to the same set changes nothing.)
- $(s|_V)|_W = s|_W$ for all $W \subseteq V \subseteq U$ and $s \in F(U)$. (Restriction is transitive.)

When $\mathcal{C} = \mathbf{Ab}$ or $\mathcal{C} = \mathbf{Vect}_k$, the restriction maps are *homomorphisms*, which means they respect the algebraic structure:

$$
(s + t)|_V = s|_V + t|_V, \qquad (\lambda s)|_V = \lambda(s|_V).
$$

This is not an additional axiom — it is a consequence of the fact that $\operatorname{res}_{U,V}$ is a morphism in $\mathcal{C}$.

### The Global Sections Functor

The set $F(X)$ — the sections over the entire space — is called the set of **global sections** of $F$. It is often denoted $\Gamma(X, F)$ or $H^0(X, F)$. The functor $\Gamma(X, -): \mathbf{PSh}(X) \to \mathcal{C}$ that sends a presheaf to its global sections is one of the most important functors in all of algebraic geometry. Its derived functors are the sheaf cohomology groups $H^n(X, F)$, which measure the obstruction to extending local sections to global ones.

## 17.5 Morphisms of Presheaves: Natural Transformations

Having defined the objects (presheaves), we must define the morphisms between them.

**Definition 17.2 (Morphism of Presheaves).** Let $F, G: \operatorname{Op}(X)^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathcal{C}$ be two presheaves on $X$. A **morphism of presheaves** $\varphi: F \to G$ is a natural transformation from $F$ to $G$. Concretely, $\varphi$ consists of:

- For every open set $U \subseteq X$, a morphism $\varphi_U: F(U) \to G(U)$ in $\mathcal{C}$.

These morphisms must satisfy the **naturality condition**: for every inclusion $V \subseteq U$, the following diagram commutes:

$$
\begin{array}{ccc}
F(U) & \xrightarrow{\varphi_U} & G(U) \\
\downarrow{\operatorname{res}^F_{U,V}} & & \downarrow{\operatorname{res}^G_{U,V}} \\
F(V) & \xrightarrow{\varphi_V} & G(V)
\end{array}
$$

In other words, for every section $s \in F(U)$:

$$
\varphi_V(s|_V) = \varphi_U(s)|_V.
$$

The naturality condition says that "applying the morphism and then restricting" is the same as "restricting and then applying the morphism." This is exactly the commutativity condition for natural transformations from Chapter 15, specialized to the functor category over $\operatorname{Op}(X)^{\mathrm{op}}$.

> **Insight.** The naturality condition is not a bureaucratic formality. It ensures that a morphism of presheaves respects the *local-to-global structure* of the presheaves. A collection of maps $\varphi_U$ that fails naturality would be a family of transformations that are incompatible with the way local data is related across different open sets. Naturality is the mathematical expression of *coherence*.

### Example: Differentiation

Consider $X = \mathbb{R}$ and two presheaves of abelian groups: $\mathcal{C}^\infty$, where $\mathcal{C}^\infty(U)$ is the additive group of smooth functions on $U$, and $\mathcal{C}^\infty$ again with the same definition. The derivative operator

$$
d_U: \mathcal{C}^\infty(U) \to \mathcal{C}^\infty(U), \quad f \mapsto f'
$$

defines a morphism of presheaves $d: \mathcal{C}^\infty \to \mathcal{C}^\infty$. The naturality condition states that for $V \subseteq U$ and $f \in \mathcal{C}^\infty(U)$:

$$
(f|_V)' = (f')|_V.
$$

This is exactly the familiar fact that "the derivative of the restriction is the restriction of the derivative" — a statement so obvious that one rarely pauses to notice it is a naturality condition.

## 17.6 The Category of Presheaves $\mathbf{PSh}(X)$

**Definition 17.3.** Let $X$ be a topological space and $\mathcal{C}$ a category. The **category of presheaves on $X$ with values in $\mathcal{C}$**, denoted $\mathbf{PSh}(X, \mathcal{C})$ or simply $\mathbf{PSh}(X)$ when $\mathcal{C}$ is understood, is the functor category:

$$
\mathbf{PSh}(X) = \operatorname{Fun}(\operatorname{Op}(X)^{\mathrm{op}}, \mathcal{C}) = [\operatorname{Op}(X)^{\mathrm{op}}, \mathcal{C}].
$$

- **Objects** are presheaves $F: \operatorname{Op}(X)^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathcal{C}$.
- **Morphisms** are natural transformations between presheaves.
- **Composition** is vertical composition of natural transformations (as defined in Chapter 15).
- **Identities** are identity natural transformations.

This is a powerful observation. The category $\mathbf{PSh}(X)$ inherits many good properties from $\mathcal{C}$:

1. **If $\mathcal{C}$ is an abelian category, so is $\mathbf{PSh}(X)$.** Kernels, cokernels, images, and exact sequences of presheaves are all computed "open-set by open-set" (i.e., pointwise). A sequence $0 \to F \to G \to H \to 0$ is exact in $\mathbf{PSh}(X)$ if and only if $0 \to F(U) \to G(U) \to H(U) \to 0$ is exact in $\mathcal{C}$ for every open set $U$.

2. **$\mathbf{PSh}(X)$ has all limits and colimits** (when $\mathcal{C}$ does), and they are computed pointwise. For example, the product of two presheaves $F$ and $G$ is the presheaf $(F \times G)(U) = F(U) \times G(U)$.

3. **$\mathbf{PSh}(X)$ has enough injectives** (when $\mathcal{C} = \mathbf{Ab}$), which is the technical condition needed to define sheaf cohomology via derived functors.

> **Why This Matters.** The fact that presheaf operations are computed pointwise is a tremendous simplification. It means that, at the presheaf level, there are no subtle global obstructions — everything reduces to algebra in the target category $\mathcal{C}$. The subtlety enters only when we pass to sheaves, where the gluing axiom introduces genuinely global constraints.

## 17.7 Examples Worked in Detail

We now construct several important presheaves in complete detail, verifying all axioms.

### 17.7.1 The Constant Presheaf

Let $A$ be an object of $\mathcal{C}$. The **constant presheaf** $\underline{A}^{\mathrm{pre}}$ on $X$ is defined by:

- $\underline{A}^{\mathrm{pre}}(U) = A$ for every nonempty open set $U \subseteq X$.
- $\underline{A}^{\mathrm{pre}}(\emptyset) = \{*\}$ (a terminal object, or simply $A$ in some conventions).
- $\operatorname{res}_{U,V} = \operatorname{id}_A$ for every inclusion $V \subseteq U$ of nonempty open sets.

**Verification of axioms.** (P1): $\operatorname{res}_{U,U} = \operatorname{id}_A = \operatorname{id}_{F(U)}$. (P2): $\operatorname{res}_{V,W} \circ \operatorname{res}_{U,V} = \operatorname{id}_A \circ \operatorname{id}_A = \operatorname{id}_A = \operatorname{res}_{U,W}$. Both axioms hold trivially.

> **Warning.** The constant presheaf is generally *not* a sheaf. Consider $X = \mathbb{R}$, $A = \mathbb{Z}$, and two disjoint open intervals $U_1 = (0,1)$ and $U_2 = (2,3)$. Their union $U = U_1 \cup U_2$ is open. The constant presheaf assigns $\underline{\mathbb{Z}}^{\mathrm{pre}}(U) = \mathbb{Z}$, but the sheaf condition would require $F(U) \cong F(U_1) \times F(U_2) = \mathbb{Z} \times \mathbb{Z}$, since sections on disjoint open sets are independent. The constant presheaf fails to detect the disconnectedness of $U$. The *constant sheaf* $\underline{A}$ (its sheafification) assigns locally constant functions $U \to A$, which does detect connected components.

### 17.7.2 The Presheaf of Continuous Functions

Let $X$ be a topological space and $Y$ a fixed topological space (often $Y = \mathbb{R}$ or $Y = \mathbb{C}$). Define:

$$
\mathcal{C}^0_Y(U) = \{ f: U \to Y \mid f \text{ is continuous} \}.
$$

For $V \subseteq U$, the restriction map is literal restriction of functions:

$$
\operatorname{res}_{U,V}(f) = f|_V,
$$

where $f|_V: V \to Y$ is the function $f$ with its domain restricted to $V$.

**Verification of axioms.**

- (P1): $f|_U = f$ for all $f: U \to Y$. Trivially true.
- (P2): $(f|_V)|_W = f|_W$ for all $W \subseteq V \subseteq U$. This is a basic property of function restriction.

When $Y = \mathbb{R}$, the set $\mathcal{C}^0(U) = \mathcal{C}^0_{\mathbb{R}}(U)$ carries a natural ring structure under pointwise addition and multiplication: $(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x)$ and $(f \cdot g)(x) = f(x) \cdot g(x)$. The restriction maps are ring homomorphisms, so $\mathcal{C}^0$ is a presheaf of rings. This is the prototypical example of a **structure presheaf** — and, as we will see in Chapter 18, it is in fact a *sheaf*.

### 17.7.3 The Presheaf of Sections of a Bundle

Let $\pi: E \to X$ be a continuous map (think of a fiber bundle, a vector bundle, or a covering space). The **presheaf of sections** $\Gamma_E$ is defined by:

$$
\Gamma_E(U) = \{ s: U \to E \mid s \text{ is continuous and } \pi \circ s = \operatorname{id}_U \}.
$$

A section $s \in \Gamma_E(U)$ is a continuous map that assigns to each point $x \in U$ an element $s(x)$ in the fiber $\pi^{-1}(x)$, in a continuous fashion. Geometrically, a section is a "continuous choice of one element from each fiber."

For $V \subseteq U$, the restriction $s|_V$ is again a section over $V$: if $\pi \circ s = \operatorname{id}_U$, then $\pi \circ (s|_V) = \operatorname{id}_V$. The presheaf axioms follow from the properties of function restriction, exactly as in the previous example.

When $E$ is a vector bundle, the fibers $\pi^{-1}(x)$ are vector spaces, and $\Gamma_E(U)$ inherits a vector space structure via pointwise operations. Thus $\Gamma_E$ becomes a presheaf of vector spaces — a presheaf valued in $\mathbf{Vect}_k$. This is the bridge between the bundle perspective of Chapter 4 and the presheaf perspective of this chapter:

$$
\boxed{\text{Vector bundles} \longleftrightarrow \text{Locally free sheaves of } \mathcal{O}_X\text{-modules}}
$$

This correspondence (the Serre--Swan theorem and its relatives) is one of the great unifying results of modern geometry.

## 17.8 Presheaves on a Basis

In practice, it is often inconvenient to specify a presheaf on *all* open sets. Many topological spaces have a **basis** $\mathcal{B}$ for their topology — a collection of open sets such that every open set is a union of members of $\mathcal{B}$ — and it suffices to define the presheaf on the basis.

**Definition 17.4.** Let $\mathcal{B}$ be a basis for the topology on $X$. A **presheaf on the basis $\mathcal{B}$** consists of:

- For every $B \in \mathcal{B}$, an object $F(B)$ in $\mathcal{C}$.
- For every inclusion $B' \subseteq B$ with $B, B' \in \mathcal{B}$, a restriction morphism $\operatorname{res}_{B, B'}: F(B) \to F(B')$.
- The identity and composition axioms hold for basis elements.

**Proposition 17.5.** Every presheaf on a basis $\mathcal{B}$ extends uniquely (up to canonical isomorphism) to a presheaf on $\operatorname{Op}(X)$, provided the presheaf satisfies a compatibility condition for overlapping basis elements.

The extension is constructed as follows. For an arbitrary open set $U = \bigcup_{\alpha} B_\alpha$ (where $B_\alpha \in \mathcal{B}$), we define $F(U)$ as a suitable limit (in the categorical sense) of the $F(B_\alpha)$. The precise construction involves the theory of Kan extensions, which we met briefly in Chapter 15.

### Example: Presheaves on $\mathbb{R}^n$

The standard basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$ consists of open balls $B(x, r)$. To define a presheaf on $\mathbb{R}^n$, it suffices to specify the sections on open balls and the restriction maps between them. This is a significant simplification: instead of dealing with arbitrary open subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$ (which can be topologically wild), we work only with the well-behaved open balls.

## 17.9 Stalks: The Colimit Construction

One of the deepest ideas in sheaf theory is that of the **stalk** — the collection of all "infinitesimally local" data at a single point. The stalk captures what the presheaf "knows" about the immediate neighborhood of a point, stripped of any information about distant regions.

**Definition 17.6 (Stalk).** Let $F$ be a presheaf on $X$ and let $x \in X$. The **stalk of $F$ at $x$** is the colimit

$$
F_x = \varinjlim_{U \ni x} F(U),
$$

taken over all open sets $U$ containing $x$, ordered by reverse inclusion (i.e., in the directed system where $V \leq U$ when $V \subseteq U$, and the transition maps are the restriction maps $\operatorname{res}_{U,V}$).

Let us unpack this definition. The open neighborhoods of $x$ form a directed system: for any two neighborhoods $U_1, U_2$ of $x$, the intersection $U_1 \cap U_2$ is again a neighborhood of $x$ and is contained in both. The colimit of this directed system is computed as follows.

**Concrete construction (for $\mathcal{C} = \mathbf{Set}$ or $\mathcal{C} = \mathbf{Ab}$).** Consider the disjoint union

$$
\coprod_{U \ni x} F(U) = \{ (U, s) : x \in U, \, s \in F(U) \}.
$$

Define an equivalence relation: $(U, s) \sim (V, t)$ if there exists an open set $W$ with $x \in W \subseteq U \cap V$ such that

$$
s|_W = t|_W.
$$

Two sections are equivalent if they "agree near $x$" — if their restrictions to some common neighborhood of $x$ coincide. The stalk is the set of equivalence classes:

$$
F_x = \coprod_{U \ni x} F(U) \Big/ \!\sim.
$$

When $\mathcal{C} = \mathbf{Ab}$, the stalk inherits an abelian group structure from the groups $F(U)$: the sum of two equivalence classes $[U, s]$ and $[V, t]$ is defined by choosing a common neighborhood $W \subseteq U \cap V$ and setting $[U, s] + [V, t] = [W, s|_W + t|_W]$. This is well-defined precisely because the colimit is a filtered colimit, and filtered colimits commute with finite limits in $\mathbf{Ab}$.

> **Intuition.** The stalk $F_x$ is the "microscope" of the presheaf at $x$. It retains all the information that $F$ carries in *arbitrarily small* neighborhoods of $x$, but forgets everything about distant points. Two sections that differ far from $x$ but agree near $x$ become identified in the stalk. The stalk is a purely local invariant.

### The Canonical Map to the Stalk

For each open neighborhood $U$ of $x$, there is a canonical map

$$
\rho_{U,x}: F(U) \to F_x, \quad s \mapsto [U, s] =: s_x.
$$

The image $s_x$ is called the **germ** of $s$ at $x$.

## 17.10 Germs: Equivalence Classes of Local Data

**Definition 17.7 (Germ).** Let $F$ be a presheaf on $X$, $x \in X$, and $s \in F(U)$ for some open $U \ni x$. The **germ of $s$ at $x$** is the equivalence class

$$
\operatorname{germ}_x(s) = s_x = [(U, s)] \in F_x.
$$

Two sections $s \in F(U)$ and $t \in F(V)$ have the same germ at $x$ if and only if they agree on some neighborhood of $x$ contained in $U \cap V$.

### Example: Germs of Smooth Functions

Let $F = \mathcal{C}^\infty$ be the presheaf of smooth functions on $\mathbb{R}$, and let $x = 0$. The stalk $\mathcal{C}^\infty_0$ consists of germs of smooth functions at the origin. Two smooth functions $f$ and $g$, possibly defined on different open neighborhoods of $0$, determine the same germ at $0$ if they agree on some open interval containing $0$.

The stalk $\mathcal{C}^\infty_0$ is a local ring: its unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}_0$ consists of germs of functions vanishing at $0$. The quotient $\mathcal{C}^\infty_0 / \mathfrak{m}_0 \cong \mathbb{R}$ recovers the value of the function at $0$. The ring structure of $\mathcal{C}^\infty_0$ encodes not just the value of a function at $0$, but all its derivatives — all the Taylor coefficients — in a coordinate-free manner.

### Example: Germs of Holomorphic Functions

For $F = \mathcal{O}$ (the presheaf of holomorphic functions on $\mathbb{C}$) and $x = 0$, the stalk $\mathcal{O}_0$ consists of convergent power series $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} a_n z^n$. This is the ring of convergent power series $\mathbb{C}\{z\}$, a fundamental object in complex analysis and algebraic geometry. Two holomorphic functions define the same germ at $0$ if and only if they have the same Taylor series at $0$.

> **Connection to Algebra.** The stalk of the structure sheaf at a point is the local ring that governs the "infinitesimal geometry" at that point. This is the bridge between sheaf theory and commutative algebra — a bridge that Grothendieck crossed to revolutionary effect in the foundations of algebraic geometry.

## 17.11 The Etale Space of a Presheaf

There is a dual perspective on presheaves that transforms functorial data into geometric data. Given a presheaf $F$ on $X$, we construct a topological space — the **espace etale** (or **etale space**) — that encodes the same information as the presheaf but in a spatial, geometric form.

**Construction.** Define the set

$$
\operatorname{Et}(F) = \coprod_{x \in X} F_x,
$$

the disjoint union of all stalks. There is a natural projection map $p: \operatorname{Et}(F) \to X$ that sends each germ $s_x \in F_x$ to the point $x$.

We equip $\operatorname{Et}(F)$ with a topology as follows. For each open set $U \subseteq X$ and each section $s \in F(U)$, define

$$
\widetilde{s}(U) = \{ s_x \in F_x : x \in U \} \subseteq \operatorname{Et}(F).
$$

The sets $\widetilde{s}(U)$, as $U$ ranges over all open sets and $s$ ranges over all sections of $F(U)$, form a basis for a topology on $\operatorname{Et}(F)$.

**Key properties:**

1. The projection $p: \operatorname{Et}(F) \to X$ is a **local homeomorphism**: every point of $\operatorname{Et}(F)$ has a neighborhood that $p$ maps homeomorphically onto an open subset of $X$.
2. The sections of the etale space (continuous maps $s: U \to \operatorname{Et}(F)$ with $p \circ s = \operatorname{id}_U$) recover the **sheafification** of $F$ — the "closest" sheaf to $F$.
3. The fibers $p^{-1}(x) = F_x$ are the stalks of $F$, equipped with the discrete topology.

> **Remark.** The etale space construction establishes an equivalence between presheaves (functorial, algebraic objects) and local homeomorphisms over $X$ (geometric, spatial objects). This duality between the algebraic and the geometric is a recurring theme in mathematics, and it reaches its fullest expression in Grothendieck's theory of topoi.

We will not develop the etale space construction in full detail here, as our main path to sheaf theory in Chapter 18 proceeds through the functorial approach. But the reader should be aware that this geometric perspective exists and provides valuable intuition: a presheaf is a "space over $X$" whose fiber at each point is the stalk, and a section of the presheaf is a continuous way of choosing one element from each fiber.

## 17.13 Why This Matters for Reaching Sheaf Theory

We have constructed an elaborate and precise framework: categories of open sets, contravariant functors, natural transformations, stalks, germs. The reader may reasonably ask: *Why go to all this trouble? What can a sheaf do that a presheaf cannot?*

The answer lies in one word: **gluing**.

A presheaf assigns data to open sets and provides restriction maps, but it makes no promise about the *converse* process. Given local data on an open cover $\{U_i\}$ that is compatible on overlaps (i.e., $s_i|_{U_i \cap U_j} = s_j|_{U_i \cap U_j}$), can we always find a global section $s \in F(U)$ that restricts to each $s_i$? A presheaf does not guarantee this. A **sheaf** does.

More precisely, a sheaf is a presheaf that satisfies two additional axioms:

**(Locality / Separation).** If two sections $s, t \in F(U)$ satisfy $s|_{U_i} = t|_{U_i}$ for all $i$, then $s = t$. Global sections are determined by their local restrictions.

**(Gluing).** If sections $s_i \in F(U_i)$ satisfy $s_i|_{U_i \cap U_j} = s_j|_{U_i \cap U_j}$ for all $i, j$, then there exists a section $s \in F(U)$ with $s|_{U_i} = s_i$ for all $i$. Compatible local data can be assembled into global data.

Together, these say that the sequence

$$
0 \to F(U) \xrightarrow{\prod \operatorname{res}} \prod_i F(U_i) \rightrightarrows \prod_{i,j} F(U_i \cap U_j)
$$

is an **equalizer diagram**. This is a clean, categorical formulation — and it is only possible because we have set up the presheaf framework with such care.

> **The Punchline.** A presheaf is a rule for assigning local data and restricting it. A sheaf is a presheaf where local data, when compatible, can be uniquely assembled into global data. Presheaves are "almost sheaves" — they lack only the gluing axiom. Everything we have built in this chapter is the scaffolding upon which the sheaf condition (Chapter 18) will be erected.

Let us preview why this matters with a concrete example. Consider two continuous functions $f: U_1 \to \mathbb{R}$ and $g: U_2 \to \mathbb{R}$ that agree on the overlap: $f(x) = g(x)$ for all $x \in U_1 \cap U_2$. Then the function

$$
h(x) = \begin{cases} f(x) & \text{if } x \in U_1, \\ g(x) & \text{if } x \in U_2 \end{cases}
$$

is well-defined and continuous on $U_1 \cup U_2$. This is the gluing axiom in action for the presheaf of continuous functions — and it is the reason that $\mathcal{C}^0$ is not merely a presheaf but a genuine sheaf. In contrast, the constant presheaf $\underline{A}^{\mathrm{pre}}$ (Section 17.7.1) fails gluing: you can choose different constants on disconnected components, but the constant presheaf has no global section that restricts to these different constants.

The entire apparatus of sheaf cohomology, which measures the obstruction to extending local data to global data, relies on the precise functorial framework we have established here. Without the categorical language, each cohomological computation would require ad hoc arguments. With it, we have a machine.

## 17.14 What to Carry Forward

As you proceed to Chapter 18, keep the following definitions and results at your fingertips:

1. **$\operatorname{Op}(X)$ is a poset category.** Its objects are open sets; its morphisms are inclusions. This is the domain category for all presheaves on $X$.

2. **A presheaf is a contravariant functor $F: \operatorname{Op}(X)^{\mathrm{op}} \to \mathcal{C}$.** The two presheaf axioms are the two functoriality axioms: preservation of identities and preservation of composition.

3. **Morphisms of presheaves are natural transformations.** The naturality condition ensures compatibility with restriction maps.

4. **$\mathbf{PSh}(X)$ is the functor category $[\operatorname{Op}(X)^{\mathrm{op}}, \mathcal{C}]$.** It inherits limits, colimits, and exactness properties from $\mathcal{C}$.

5. **The stalk $F_x = \varinjlim_{U \ni x} F(U)$ is a filtered colimit.** It captures the infinitesimally local data of $F$ at the point $x$. A morphism of presheaves $\varphi: F \to G$ induces maps on stalks $\varphi_x: F_x \to G_x$, and many properties of $\varphi$ (injectivity, surjectivity) can be checked stalk by stalk.

6. **A germ is an equivalence class of sections** that agree near a point. Two sections have the same germ at $x$ if they coincide on some neighborhood of $x$.

7. **The constant presheaf $\underline{A}^{\mathrm{pre}}$ is generally not a sheaf.** This is the canonical example showing that the presheaf axioms alone are insufficient for the local-to-global principle.

8. **The etale space $\operatorname{Et}(F)$ provides a geometric dual to the functorial definition.** Its sections recover the sheafification of $F$.

9. **A sheaf is a presheaf plus gluing.** The gluing axiom (locality + gluing) is the single additional condition that transforms a presheaf into a sheaf. Chapter 18 will develop this in full.

> **Final Reflection.** We began Chapter 16 with stories and pictures. We end Chapter 17 with definitions and theorems. The passage from intuition to formalism is one of the deepest skills in mathematics — not because formalism is superior to intuition, but because *each needs the other*. Intuition without formalism is imprecise; formalism without intuition is blind. The presheaf, as a contravariant functor, is a triumph of formalism: a single sentence that encodes a universe of geometric meaning. Carry both the sentence and the meaning forward.
