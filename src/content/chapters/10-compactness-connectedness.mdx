# Chapter 10: Compactness, Connectedness, and Global Behavior

In Chapters 8 and 9, we assembled the local vocabulary of topology: open sets, neighborhoods, bases, and coverings. We learned how to describe what happens "near" a point. But the deepest questions in mathematics are rarely local. Does a continuous function on a surface attain its maximum? Can a space be torn into two separate pieces without cutting through an open set? Can we always patch local data into a coherent global object?

These questions demand *global* properties—properties that cannot be detected by examining any single neighborhood, but emerge only when we consider the space as a whole. The two paramount global properties in all of topology are **compactness** and **connectedness**. The first governs finiteness: it guarantees that infinite local data can always be reduced to finite data. The second governs wholeness: it ensures that the space cannot be decomposed into disjoint pieces. Together, they form the bridge between the local world of open sets and the global world of sheaves, bundles, and cohomology.

## Guiding Idea of the Chapter

> **From local data to global conclusions.** Compactness ensures that every open cover—no matter how wildly infinite—can be tamed into a finite subcover. Connectedness ensures that a space is "all one piece," so that local information propagates across the entire domain. These two properties are the fundamental mechanisms by which topology converts local observations into global theorems.

The ultimate destination is sheaf theory. A sheaf is precisely a device for organizing local data (sections over open sets) and gluing it into global data (global sections). The success of this gluing depends critically on the global structure of the underlying space. Compactness guarantees that we need only finitely many local pieces. Connectedness guarantees that these pieces cannot drift apart into isolated islands. And paracompactness—a refinement we shall meet at the chapter's end—guarantees the existence of partitions of unity, the indispensable tool that makes gluing rigorous.

## 10.1 Compact Spaces: Every Open Cover Has a Finite Subcover

### The Definition

Let $X$ be a topological space. An **open cover** of $X$ is a collection $\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$ of open sets whose union contains $X$:

$$
X \subseteq \bigcup_{\alpha \in A} U_\alpha
$$

A **subcover** is a subcollection that still covers $X$. A **finite subcover** is a subcover consisting of finitely many sets $U_{\alpha_1}, U_{\alpha_2}, \dots, U_{\alpha_n}$.

**Definition.** A topological space $X$ is **compact** if every open cover of $X$ admits a finite subcover.

This definition is deceptively simple. It says: no matter how creatively you try to cover $X$ with open sets, no matter how many you use—even uncountably many—you can always throw away all but finitely many of them and still have $X$ completely covered.

> **Geometric Intuition.** Compactness is the topological abstraction of "bounded and closed." In $\mathbb{R}^n$, those two words suffice. In general topological spaces, the correct abstraction is the finite subcover property. Think of compactness as saying: "this space is small enough that it cannot escape to infinity, and tight enough that it contains all its own boundary points."

### First Examples

1. **Finite spaces.** Every finite topological space is compact. Any open cover is itself finite.

2. **The closed interval $[0, 1]$.** This is the prototypical compact subset of $\mathbb{R}$. We shall prove this via the Heine-Borel theorem below.

3. **The open interval $(0, 1)$.** This is *not* compact. Consider the cover $\{(1/n, 1) : n \geq 2\}$. Every point of $(0, 1)$ lies in some $(1/n, 1)$, but no finite subcollection covers points arbitrarily close to $0$.

4. **The real line $\mathbb{R}$.** Not compact. The cover $\{(-n, n) : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$ has no finite subcover.

5. **The circle $S^1$.** Compact. Intuitively, it is "closed and bounded" when embedded in $\mathbb{R}^2$.

### Properties of Compact Spaces

Compactness propagates through the standard topological constructions in predictable ways:

- **Closed subsets of compact spaces are compact.** If $X$ is compact and $C \subseteq X$ is closed, then $C$ is compact. The proof is elegant: given an open cover of $C$, adjoin the open set $X \setminus C$ to obtain an open cover of $X$; extract a finite subcover; discard $X \setminus C$.

- **Compact subsets of Hausdorff spaces are closed.** In a Hausdorff space, compactness and closedness are intimately linked. Every compact subset is automatically closed.

- **Finite unions of compact sets are compact.** Combine the finite subcovers.

- **Arbitrary intersections of compact subsets of a Hausdorff space are compact.** They are closed subsets of a compact set.

- **Products of compact spaces are compact (Tychonoff's Theorem).** This is one of the deepest results in general topology. The finite case is elementary; the general case requires the Axiom of Choice (or equivalently, Zorn's Lemma). The product $\prod_{\alpha \in A} X_\alpha$ is compact in the product topology if and only if each $X_\alpha$ is compact.

> **Why Tychonoff Matters.** Tychonoff's theorem is the engine behind many existence results in functional analysis, probability theory, and algebraic geometry. It guarantees, for instance, that the space of all possible "states" of an infinite system is compact—a fact that underpins the existence of equilibria, ultrafilters, and Stone-Cech compactifications.

## 10.2 The Heine-Borel Theorem

The most important compactness result in classical analysis gives a complete characterization of compact subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n$.

**Theorem (Heine-Borel).** A subset $K \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded.

The proof in one direction is straightforward: a compact subset of a Hausdorff space is closed, and if $K$ were unbounded, the cover $\{B(\mathbf{0}, n) : n \in \mathbb{N}\}$ would have no finite subcover.

The converse is the deep direction. The classical proof proceeds as follows:

1. A closed and bounded subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is contained in some box $[-M, M]^n$.
2. The box $[-M, M]^n = [-M, M] \times \cdots \times [-M, M]$ is a product of compact intervals.
3. Each interval $[-M, M]$ is compact (proved by the bisection argument or the least upper bound property of $\mathbb{R}$).
4. By Tychonoff's theorem (or its finite-product special case), the box is compact.
5. $K$ is a closed subset of a compact set, hence compact.

**The Bisection Argument for $[a, b]$.** Suppose $\{U_\alpha\}$ covers $[a, b]$ with no finite subcover. Bisect the interval into $[a, (a+b)/2]$ and $[(a+b)/2, b]$. At least one half has no finite subcover. Repeat. By the Nested Interval Theorem, the nested sequence of closed intervals converges to a single point $x^*$. But $x^*$ lies in some $U_\alpha$, which is open, so $U_\alpha$ alone covers some neighborhood of $x^*$—and hence covers all but finitely many of the nested intervals. This contradicts the assumption that none had a finite subcover.

> **A Subtle Warning.** Heine-Borel is specific to $\mathbb{R}^n$. In an arbitrary metric space, "closed and bounded" does not imply compact. Consider the closed unit ball in an infinite-dimensional Banach space: it is closed and bounded but never compact. The correct generalization to metric spaces involves total boundedness.

## 10.3 Compactness and Continuous Functions

Compactness is most powerful when combined with continuity. The following results are the workhorses of analysis and topology.

**Theorem (Image of a Compact Set).** If $f: X \to Y$ is continuous and $X$ is compact, then $f(X)$ is compact.

*Proof.* Let $\{V_\alpha\}$ be an open cover of $f(X)$. Then $\{f^{-1}(V_\alpha)\}$ is an open cover of $X$. By compactness, extract a finite subcover $\{f^{-1}(V_{\alpha_1}), \dots, f^{-1}(V_{\alpha_n})\}$. Then $\{V_{\alpha_1}, \dots, V_{\alpha_n}\}$ covers $f(X)$.

**Theorem (Extreme Value Theorem).** If $f: X \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous and $X$ is compact, then $f$ attains its maximum and minimum. That is, there exist points $x_{\max}, x_{\min} \in X$ such that $f(x_{\min}) \leq f(x) \leq f(x_{\max})$ for all $x \in X$.

*Proof.* The image $f(X)$ is a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}$, hence closed and bounded by Heine-Borel. Being bounded, the supremum $M = \sup f(X)$ exists. Being closed, $M \in f(X)$, so some $x_{\max}$ maps to $M$. Similarly for the infimum.

**Theorem (Closed Map Lemma).** If $f: X \to Y$ is a continuous bijection, $X$ is compact, and $Y$ is Hausdorff, then $f$ is a homeomorphism.

This result is remarkable: to verify that a continuous bijection is a homeomorphism, it suffices to check compactness of the domain and the Hausdorff property of the codomain. No explicit verification of the continuity of $f^{-1}$ is needed.

**Theorem (Uniform Continuity).** If $f: X \to Y$ is continuous, $X$ is a compact metric space, and $Y$ is a metric space, then $f$ is uniformly continuous.

This is the Heine-Cantor theorem. Compactness upgrades pointwise continuity to uniform continuity—a dramatic strengthening that eliminates the dependence of the $\delta$ on the point $x$.

## 10.4 Sequential Compactness and Equivalences in Metric Spaces

In metric spaces, compactness has several equivalent formulations that are often more convenient in practice.

**Definition.** A topological space $X$ is **sequentially compact** if every sequence in $X$ has a convergent subsequence.

**Definition.** A metric space $(X, d)$ is **totally bounded** if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a finite set $\{x_1, \dots, x_n\} \subseteq X$ such that $X = \bigcup_{i=1}^n B(x_i, \varepsilon)$.

**Theorem (Equivalences in Metric Spaces).** For a metric space $(X, d)$, the following are equivalent:

1. $X$ is compact (every open cover has a finite subcover).
2. $X$ is sequentially compact (every sequence has a convergent subsequence).
3. $X$ is complete and totally bounded.

The equivalence of (1) and (2) fails in general topological spaces. Sequential compactness and compactness diverge in non-metrizable spaces. For instance, the product $\{0, 1\}^{\mathbb{R}}$ (with the product topology) is compact by Tychonoff but not sequentially compact.

**The Bolzano-Weierstrass Property.** In $\mathbb{R}^n$, sequential compactness is equivalent to being closed and bounded—every bounded sequence in $\mathbb{R}^n$ has a convergent subsequence. This classical result is the sequential incarnation of Heine-Borel.

> **Practical Significance.** In applications—optimization, numerical analysis, machine learning—sequential compactness is often the most useful formulation. When you need a convergent subsequence from a minimizing sequence, you invoke compactness. The extreme value theorem, gradient convergence guarantees, and the existence of optimal solutions all rest on this pillar.

## 10.5 Connected Spaces: No Nontrivial Clopen Sets

We now turn to the second great global property. Where compactness governs finiteness, connectedness governs wholeness.

**Definition.** A topological space $X$ is **connected** if it cannot be written as a union of two nonempty, disjoint open sets. Equivalently, $X$ is connected if the only subsets of $X$ that are both open and closed (called **clopen** sets) are $\emptyset$ and $X$ itself.

**Definition.** A **disconnection** of $X$ is a pair of nonempty disjoint open sets $U, V$ with $X = U \cup V$. A space is connected if and only if no disconnection exists.

### Examples

1. **$\mathbb{R}$ is connected.** Suppose $\mathbb{R} = U \cup V$ with $U, V$ open, nonempty, disjoint. Pick $a \in U$ and $b \in V$ with $a < b$. Let $c = \sup(U \cap [a, b])$. Then $c$ can belong to neither $U$ nor $V$ without contradiction, using the openness of both sets. This proof relies essentially on the completeness of $\mathbb{R}$.

2. **$\mathbb{Q}$ is not connected.** The sets $\{q \in \mathbb{Q} : q < \sqrt{2}\}$ and $\{q \in \mathbb{Q} : q > \sqrt{2}\}$ form a disconnection.

3. **The interval $[a, b]$ is connected.** The same supremum argument applies.

4. **$\mathbb{R}^n$ is connected for all $n \geq 1$.** It is path-connected (see below), hence connected.

5. **$S^1$ is connected.** It is the continuous image of the connected space $[0, 2\pi]$ under the map $t \mapsto (\cos t, \sin t)$.

### Properties of Connected Spaces

- **Continuous images of connected spaces are connected.** If $f: X \to Y$ is continuous and $X$ is connected, then $f(X)$ is connected. This gives us the Intermediate Value Theorem as an immediate corollary.

- **The Intermediate Value Theorem.** If $f: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous and $f(a) < c < f(b)$, then there exists $x \in (a, b)$ with $f(x) = c$. The image $f([a, b])$ is a connected subset of $\mathbb{R}$, hence an interval.

- **Unions of connected sets with a common point are connected.** If $\{C_\alpha\}$ is a family of connected subsets of $X$ with $\bigcap_\alpha C_\alpha \neq \emptyset$, then $\bigcup_\alpha C_\alpha$ is connected.

- **Closures of connected sets are connected.** If $C$ is connected, then $\overline{C}$ is connected.

- **Products of connected spaces are connected.** The product $\prod_\alpha X_\alpha$ is connected if and only if each factor $X_\alpha$ is connected.

## 10.6 Path-Connectedness

Connectedness says a space is "one piece" in a topological sense. Path-connectedness provides a stronger, more intuitive formulation.

**Definition.** A space $X$ is **path-connected** if for every pair of points $x, y \in X$, there exists a continuous map $\gamma: [0, 1] \to X$ with $\gamma(0) = x$ and $\gamma(1) = y$. The map $\gamma$ is called a **path** from $x$ to $y$.

**Theorem.** Every path-connected space is connected.

*Proof.* Suppose $X$ is path-connected but not connected. Then $X = U \cup V$ with $U, V$ open, nonempty, disjoint. Pick $x \in U$, $y \in V$, and a path $\gamma: [0, 1] \to X$ from $x$ to $y$. Then $\gamma^{-1}(U)$ and $\gamma^{-1}(V)$ form a disconnection of $[0, 1]$, contradicting the connectedness of $[0, 1]$.

**The converse is false.** The **topologist's sine curve**—the closure of the graph of $\sin(1/x)$ for $x > 0$ in $\mathbb{R}^2$—is connected but not path-connected. The graph oscillates infinitely as $x \to 0^+$, and the limit segment $\{0\} \times [-1, 1]$ is connected to the graph in the topological sense but unreachable by any continuous path of finite length from the oscillating part.

> **Why the Distinction Matters.** In algebraic topology, path-connectedness is the operationally useful notion. The fundamental group $\pi_1(X, x_0)$ requires paths. Covering space theory requires path-connected base spaces. For sheaf cohomology, connected components determine the zeroth cohomology group $H^0(X; \mathcal{F})$, but it is path-connectedness that enables the monodromy arguments essential to the theory of locally constant sheaves.

## 10.7 Connected Components

Even when a space is not connected, we can decompose it into its maximal connected pieces.

**Definition.** The **connected component** of a point $x \in X$ is the largest connected subset of $X$ containing $x$:

$$
C(x) = \bigcup \{ A \subseteq X : A \text{ is connected and } x \in A \}
$$

This is well-defined because the union of connected sets sharing a common point is connected.

**Properties.**

1. The connected components partition $X$: every point belongs to exactly one component, and distinct components are disjoint.
2. Each connected component is closed (as the closure of a connected set is connected).
3. Connected components need not be open. In $\mathbb{Q}$ with the subspace topology from $\mathbb{R}$, every connected component is a single point $\{q\}$, which is not open.
4. A space is connected if and only if it has exactly one connected component.

Similarly, one defines **path components** as maximal path-connected subsets. These also partition the space. Every path component is contained in a connected component, but the inclusion may be strict (as in the topologist's sine curve).

**Definition.** A space is **totally disconnected** if every connected component is a single point. Examples: $\mathbb{Q}$, the Cantor set, the $p$-adic integers $\mathbb{Z}_p$.

## 10.8 Locally Compact and Locally Connected Spaces

Global compactness and connectedness are often too strong. Many important spaces—$\mathbb{R}^n$, manifolds, algebraic varieties—are not compact but are "compact in the neighborhood of every point."

**Definition.** A space $X$ is **locally compact** if every point has a compact neighborhood. More precisely, for each $x \in X$ and each open set $U$ containing $x$, there exists a compact set $K$ with $x \in \operatorname{int}(K) \subseteq K \subseteq U$.

**Examples.**

- $\mathbb{R}^n$ is locally compact: every point has a closed ball neighborhood.
- Every compact space is trivially locally compact.
- $\mathbb{Q}$ is *not* locally compact (no point has a compact neighborhood in the subspace topology).
- Infinite-dimensional Banach spaces are not locally compact.

**Theorem.** A locally compact Hausdorff space is regular, and indeed completely regular (Tychonoff). This regularity is essential for the existence of continuous bump functions, which in turn enable partitions of unity.

**Definition.** A space $X$ is **locally connected** if for every point $x$ and every open set $U$ containing $x$, there exists a connected open set $V$ with $x \in V \subseteq U$.

**Definition.** A space is **locally path-connected** if the connected open set $V$ in the above definition can be chosen to be path-connected.

**Theorem.** In a locally path-connected space, connectedness and path-connectedness coincide.

This is why the distinction between the two notions rarely surfaces in practice for "nice" spaces: manifolds, CW complexes, and simplicial complexes are all locally path-connected, so their connected components are automatically path components.

## 10.9 Compactification: One-Point and Stone-Cech

When a space fails to be compact, we can often "complete" it by adding points at infinity.

### One-Point (Alexandroff) Compactification

**Construction.** Let $X$ be a locally compact Hausdorff space that is not compact. Define $X^+ = X \cup \{\infty\}$ where $\infty$ is a new point not in $X$. Declare the open sets of $X^+$ to be:

1. All open subsets of $X$ (these do not contain $\infty$).
2. Sets of the form $(X \setminus K) \cup \{\infty\}$ where $K \subseteq X$ is compact.

**Theorem.** $X^+$ with this topology is a compact Hausdorff space, and $X$ embeds as an open dense subspace.

**Examples.**

- The one-point compactification of $\mathbb{R}$ is homeomorphic to the circle $S^1$. Wrapping the real line around and gluing the two "ends" at infinity produces a circle.
- The one-point compactification of $\mathbb{R}^2$ is homeomorphic to the sphere $S^2$. This is the **Riemann sphere** $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$ of complex analysis, where we adjoin a single point $\infty$ to the complex plane.
- The one-point compactification of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is $S^n$.

> **Algebraic Significance.** The Riemann sphere $\hat{\mathbb{C}} = \mathbb{C} \cup \{\infty\}$ is the simplest example of a compact Riemann surface. Meromorphic functions on $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$ are rational functions. The passage from $\mathbb{C}$ to $\hat{\mathbb{C}}$ is a prototype for the general compactification of algebraic varieties, and the behavior of sheaves near "infinity" is a central theme in algebraic geometry.

### Stone-Cech Compactification

The one-point compactification adds a single point. The **Stone-Cech compactification** $\beta X$ adds as many points as needed to make every bounded continuous function extendable.

**Universal Property.** For a completely regular space $X$, the Stone-Cech compactification $\beta X$ is the unique (up to homeomorphism) compact Hausdorff space containing $X$ as a dense subspace such that every continuous function $f: X \to [0, 1]$ extends to a continuous function $\bar{f}: \beta X \to [0, 1]$.

The construction is highly non-trivial: $\beta \mathbb{N}$, for instance, is an enormous space whose cardinality is $2^{2^{\aleph_0}}$. While rarely encountered in differential geometry or sheaf theory on manifolds, the Stone-Cech compactification plays a fundamental role in functional analysis (it is the spectrum of the $C^*$-algebra $C_b(X)$ of bounded continuous functions) and in set-theoretic topology.

## 10.10 Paracompactness and Partitions of Unity

We arrive at the topic most directly essential for sheaf theory and differential geometry. Paracompactness is the property that makes partitions of unity possible, and partitions of unity are the primary tool for passing from local data to global data.

### Paracompactness

**Definition.** An open cover $\{V_\beta\}_{\beta \in B}$ is a **refinement** of an open cover $\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$ if every $V_\beta$ is contained in some $U_\alpha$.

**Definition.** An open cover $\{V_\beta\}$ is **locally finite** if every point $x \in X$ has a neighborhood that intersects only finitely many of the $V_\beta$.

**Definition.** A Hausdorff space $X$ is **paracompact** if every open cover has a locally finite open refinement.

Paracompactness is weaker than compactness (compact implies paracompact, since a finite subcover is trivially locally finite) but stronger than mere topological regularity. It is precisely the condition that ensures the existence of smooth partitions of unity.

**Key Examples.**

- Every compact space is paracompact.
- Every metrizable space is paracompact (A. H. Stone's theorem, 1948). In particular, $\mathbb{R}^n$ and all metric spaces are paracompact.
- Every second-countable locally compact Hausdorff space is paracompact. This covers all manifolds (by standard definitions, manifolds are second-countable and Hausdorff).
- Every CW complex is paracompact.
- The **long line** is an example of a space that is locally compact and Hausdorff but not paracompact.

### Partitions of Unity

**Definition.** Let $\{U_\alpha\}_{\alpha \in A}$ be an open cover of $X$. A **partition of unity subordinate to this cover** is a collection of continuous functions $\{\varphi_\alpha: X \to [0, 1]\}_{\alpha \in A}$ satisfying:

1. **Support condition:** For each $\alpha$, the support $\operatorname{supp}(\varphi_\alpha) = \overline{\{x : \varphi_\alpha(x) \neq 0\}}$ is contained in $U_\alpha$.
2. **Local finiteness:** The collection $\{\operatorname{supp}(\varphi_\alpha)\}$ is locally finite.
3. **Normalization:** For every $x \in X$, we have $\sum_{\alpha \in A} \varphi_\alpha(x) = 1$.

The local finiteness condition ensures that the sum in (3) is actually a finite sum at each point, so there are no convergence issues.

**Theorem (Existence of Partitions of Unity).** Every paracompact Hausdorff space admits a partition of unity subordinate to any open cover. If the space is additionally a smooth manifold, the functions $\varphi_\alpha$ can be chosen to be smooth ($C^\infty$).

### Why Partitions of Unity Are Indispensable

Partitions of unity are the universal solvent for "local-to-global" problems:

1. **Globalizing local objects.** Suppose you have a Riemannian metric defined on each chart of a manifold. A partition of unity lets you average these local metrics into a single global Riemannian metric: $g = \sum_\alpha \varphi_\alpha \cdot g_\alpha$.

2. **Extending functions.** Local functions can be multiplied by bump functions $\varphi_\alpha$ and summed to produce global functions.

3. **Sheaf cohomology vanishing.** A sheaf $\mathcal{F}$ on a paracompact space is called **fine** if it admits partitions of unity. Fine sheaves have trivial higher cohomology: $H^k(X; \mathcal{F}) = 0$ for $k \geq 1$. The sheaf of smooth functions $C^\infty_X$ on a manifold is fine, which is why de Rham cohomology (computed via differential forms) agrees with sheaf cohomology (computed via Cech covers). This is the de Rham theorem.

4. **Constructing global sections.** Given local sections $s_\alpha \in \mathcal{F}(U_\alpha)$, one can attempt to build a global section $s = \sum_\alpha \varphi_\alpha \cdot s_\alpha$. This works when the sheaf has a module structure compatible with the ring of continuous (or smooth) functions.

> **The Deep Lesson.** Paracompactness is not merely a technical condition—it is the precise hypothesis that makes sheaf theory on manifolds computationally tractable. Without it, the passage from local to global would require far more delicate machinery. The fact that all metrizable spaces and all manifolds are paracompact is what makes differential geometry and geometric analysis possible as computational disciplines rather than merely existential ones.

### The Cech Perspective

For Cech cohomology, compactness plays a complementary role. A **good cover** of a space $X$ is an open cover $\{U_\alpha\}$ such that every finite intersection $U_{\alpha_1} \cap \cdots \cap U_{\alpha_k}$ is either empty or contractible. The Nerve Theorem states that if $\{U_\alpha\}$ is a good cover, then the Cech cohomology of the cover computes the true cohomology of $X$.

On a compact space, we can always extract a *finite* good cover, making the Cech complex a finite combinatorial object. The Cech cohomology groups $\check{H}^k(X; \mathcal{F})$ are then computed from a finite simplicial complex—the nerve of the cover—making them concretely computable.

## Why This Matters for Reaching Sheaf Theory

This chapter's material is not a detour—it is the structural foundation upon which sheaf theory stands. Three specific connections deserve emphasis.

**First: Paracompactness ensures partitions of unity.** A sheaf on a paracompact space that admits a module structure over the sheaf of continuous functions is automatically **fine**, and fine sheaves have vanishing higher cohomology. This is the reason that sheaf cohomology on manifolds is computable: the existence of smooth partitions of unity makes the sheaf of smooth functions into a fine resolution of the constant sheaf, and the resulting cohomology is de Rham cohomology.

**Second: Compactness ensures finite good covers for Cech cohomology.** When $X$ is compact, every open cover has a finite subcover, and good covers can be chosen finite. The Cech-to-derived functor spectral sequence degenerates, and Cech cohomology $\check{H}^k(X; \mathcal{F})$ coincides with derived functor cohomology $H^k(X; \mathcal{F})$. In the compact case, this cohomology is computed by a finite simplicial complex, making it accessible to explicit computation and to algorithms in topological data analysis.

**Third: Connectedness controls global sections.** For a locally constant sheaf $\mathcal{F}$ on a connected space $X$, the space of global sections $\Gamma(X, \mathcal{F})$ is isomorphic to the stalk $\mathcal{F}_x$ at any point $x$. If $X$ has $n$ connected components, then $\Gamma(X, \mathcal{F})$ is a product of $n$ stalks—one per component. Connectedness thus determines the zeroth cohomology $H^0(X; \mathcal{F})$ and governs the global behavior of locally constant (and constructible) sheaves.

> **The Unifying Principle.** Topology provides the scaffolding; sheaves provide the data. The global properties of the scaffolding—compactness, connectedness, paracompactness—determine what kind of "data management" is possible. A sheaf on a paracompact space can be studied via partitions of unity. A sheaf on a compact space can be studied via finite covers. A sheaf on a connected space has global sections controlled by its local stalks. Without these global properties, sheaf theory would be an elaborate formalism with no computational teeth.

### Chapter 10: Essential Review

- **Compactness** means every open cover has a finite subcover. It is the topological abstraction of "bounded and closed" (which is literally true in $\mathbb{R}^n$ by Heine-Borel, but requires refinement in general spaces).
- **The Extreme Value Theorem**: continuous real-valued functions on compact spaces attain their maximum and minimum. Continuous images of compact sets are compact.
- **Sequential compactness** (every sequence has a convergent subsequence) is equivalent to compactness in metric spaces, but not in general.
- **Connectedness** means no nontrivial clopen sets; the space is "one piece." **Path-connectedness** is stronger: any two points are joined by a continuous path. The two coincide for locally path-connected spaces.
- **Connected components** partition a space into maximal connected pieces. They are always closed, but not always open.
- **Local compactness and local connectedness** describe spaces that are well-behaved "near every point" without necessarily being globally compact or connected. Manifolds are locally compact and locally path-connected.
- **One-point compactification** $X^+$ adds a single point at infinity; the Riemann sphere $\hat{\mathbb{C}} = \mathbb{C}^+$ is the prototypical example. **Stone-Cech compactification** $\beta X$ is the maximal compactification, encoding all bounded continuous functions.
- **Paracompactness** (every open cover has a locally finite refinement) is the key hypothesis for **partitions of unity**. All metrizable spaces and all manifolds are paracompact.
- **Partitions of unity** subordinate to an open cover allow local constructions (metrics, sections, differential forms) to be averaged into global ones. Fine sheaves—those admitting partitions of unity—have vanishing higher cohomology.
- **For sheaf theory**: paracompactness gives partitions of unity (making fine resolutions and de Rham theory work), compactness gives finite good covers (making Cech cohomology computable), and connectedness controls global sections of locally constant sheaves.

*Proceed to Chapter 11 when the interplay between "local" and "global" feels natural—when you see compactness as a finiteness guarantee, connectedness as a propagation mechanism, and paracompactness as the passport that lets local data travel freely across an entire space.*
