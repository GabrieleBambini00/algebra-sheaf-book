# Chapter 14: Groups, Rings, Modules, and Relevant Algebraic Structures

In the first thirteen chapters of this book, we built the language of linear algebra, witnessed the power of inner products and spectral theory, entered the world of topology, constructed sheaves on graphs and cell complexes, computed cohomology, and saw how these ideas illuminate everything from gene regulation to persistent homology. Throughout all of this, there was a silent assumption operating in the background: the "coefficients" of our constructions were drawn from a *field* — typically the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ or the complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$. Vector spaces were the containers, linear maps were the morphisms, and the algebraic world was, in a precise sense, *well-behaved*.

But the road ahead — through category theory, sheaf cohomology on general topological spaces, and the full modern theory of sheaves — demands a broader algebraic vocabulary. Sheaves do not always take values in vector spaces. They take values in *modules* over *rings*, in *abelian groups*, in *graded algebras*. The stalk of a sheaf of smooth functions at a point is a *local ring*. The process of passing from a sheaf to its stalk is *localization* — a construction that makes no sense without the language of ring theory. The long exact sequences of sheaf cohomology live in categories of modules, not merely vector spaces.

This chapter provides the algebraic foundations that the remaining chapters require. We develop the theory of groups, rings, and modules from their definitions, emphasizing at every stage the structural thread that connects these abstractions to the sheaf-theoretic applications ahead.

## Guiding Idea of the Chapter

> **The central message**: Sheaves are not merely topological objects. They are *algebraic* objects living over topological spaces. A sheaf of abelian groups assigns an abelian group to each open set. A sheaf of rings assigns a ring. A sheaf of modules assigns a module. The algebraic structure carried by the sheaf determines what kind of cohomology theory it supports, what kind of exact sequences arise, and what kind of obstructions can be detected. To understand sheaves fully, we must first understand the algebraic structures they carry.

The progression of this chapter mirrors the historical development of abstract algebra itself: from groups (the simplest algebraic structure with a single operation) through rings (two operations, modeling arithmetic) to modules (the natural generalization of vector spaces when the scalars form a ring rather than a field). Each generalization introduces new phenomena — torsion, non-free modules, failure of splitting — that have no analogue in the vector space world but are essential for the geometry and topology of the chapters to come.

## 14.1 Groups: Definition, Examples, Subgroups, Homomorphisms

We begin with the most fundamental algebraic structure: the group.

**Definition.** A **group** is a set $G$ equipped with a binary operation $\cdot : G \times G \to G$ satisfying three axioms:

1. **Associativity**: For all $a, b, c \in G$, $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$.
2. **Identity element**: There exists an element $e \in G$ such that $e \cdot g = g \cdot e = g$ for all $g \in G$.
3. **Inverses**: For every $g \in G$, there exists $g^{-1} \in G$ such that $g \cdot g^{-1} = g^{-1} \cdot g = e$.

If, in addition, $a \cdot b = b \cdot a$ for all $a, b \in G$, the group is called **abelian** (or commutative). For abelian groups, the operation is typically written additively as $+$, the identity is written as $0$, and the inverse of $g$ is written as $-g$.

**Examples.** The landscape of groups is vast:

- $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the integers under addition, is an abelian group. The identity is $0$, and the inverse of $n$ is $-n$.

- $(\mathbb{R}^*, \cdot)$, the nonzero real numbers under multiplication, is an abelian group. The identity is $1$, and the inverse of $x$ is $1/x$.

- $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$, the set of all invertible $n \times n$ real matrices under matrix multiplication, is a group. It is *not* abelian for $n \geq 2$ — matrix multiplication does not commute in general. This is the **general linear group**, and it was the silent protagonist of every invertibility argument in our earlier chapters on linear algebra.

- The **symmetric group** $S_n$, consisting of all permutations of $\{1, 2, \ldots, n\}$ under composition, is a finite group of order $n!$. For $n \geq 3$, it is non-abelian.

- $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the integers modulo $n$, is a finite abelian group of order $n$. Its elements are the residue classes $\{0, 1, 2, \ldots, n-1\}$ with addition performed modulo $n$.

- The **trivial group** $\{e\}$, containing only the identity element, is a group. It is the simplest possible group and serves as the zero object in many algebraic constructions.

> **Insight**: The group axioms capture the essence of *symmetry*. Whenever a mathematical object has symmetries — rotations of a polygon, permutations of roots of a polynomial, gauge transformations of a physical field — those symmetries form a group. The study of groups is, at its core, the study of symmetry in its purest abstract form.

**Subgroups.** A subset $H \subseteq G$ is a **subgroup** of $G$ (written $H \leq G$) if $H$ is itself a group under the restriction of $G$'s operation. Equivalently, $H \leq G$ if and only if:

1. The identity $e$ of $G$ belongs to $H$.
2. If $a, b \in H$, then $a \cdot b \in H$ (closure).
3. If $a \in H$, then $a^{-1} \in H$ (closure under inverses).

For any group $G$, both $\{e\}$ and $G$ itself are subgroups. Every other subgroup is called a **proper** subgroup. In $(\mathbb{Z}, +)$, the subgroups are precisely the sets $n\mathbb{Z} = \{nk : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ for $n \geq 0$ — the multiples of $n$. This is a consequence of the fact that $\mathbb{Z}$ is a cyclic group generated by $1$.

**Homomorphisms.** The correct notion of "structure-preserving map" between groups is a group homomorphism.

**Definition.** A **group homomorphism** is a function $\varphi: G \to H$ between groups such that

$$\varphi(a \cdot_G b) = \varphi(a) \cdot_H \varphi(b)$$

for all $a, b \in G$. A homomorphism that is bijective is called an **isomorphism**, and we write $G \cong H$.

The **kernel** of a homomorphism $\varphi: G \to H$ is the set $\ker(\varphi) = \{g \in G : \varphi(g) = e_H\}$. The kernel is always a *normal* subgroup of $G$ — meaning $g \ker(\varphi) g^{-1} = \ker(\varphi)$ for all $g \in G$. This is the group-theoretic analogue of the null space of a linear map.

The **image** of $\varphi$ is $\operatorname{im}(\varphi) = \{\varphi(g) : g \in G\}$, which is always a subgroup of $H$.

The **First Isomorphism Theorem** states that $G / \ker(\varphi) \cong \operatorname{im}(\varphi)$. This is the group-theoretic analogue of the rank-nullity theorem from Chapter 1: the "size" of the domain is accounted for by the kernel and the image.

## 14.2 Group Actions and Orbits

Groups become geometrically vivid when they *act* on sets or spaces.

**Definition.** A **(left) group action** of a group $G$ on a set $X$ is a function $G \times X \to X$, written $(g, x) \mapsto g \cdot x$, satisfying:

1. $e \cdot x = x$ for all $x \in X$ (the identity acts trivially).
2. $(g \cdot h) \cdot x = g \cdot (h \cdot x)$ for all $g, h \in G$ and $x \in X$ (compatibility with group multiplication).

When $G$ acts on $X$, we say $X$ is a **$G$-set**.

**Orbits and stabilizers.** The **orbit** of a point $x \in X$ is the set $G \cdot x = \{g \cdot x : g \in G\}$ — all the points that $x$ can be moved to by elements of $G$. The **stabilizer** of $x$ is $G_x = \{g \in G : g \cdot x = x\}$ — the subgroup of elements that fix $x$.

The **Orbit-Stabilizer Theorem** provides a fundamental relationship:

$$|G \cdot x| = [G : G_x] = \frac{|G|}{|G_x|}$$

where $[G : G_x]$ is the index of $G_x$ in $G$. The size of an orbit times the size of the stabilizer equals the size of the group.

**Example: $GL_n(\mathbb{R})$ acting on $\mathbb{R}^n$.** The general linear group acts on $\mathbb{R}^n$ by matrix-vector multiplication: $A \cdot \mathbf{v} = A\mathbf{v}$. This action has exactly two orbits: $\{\mathbf{0}\}$ (every invertible matrix fixes the origin) and $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \{\mathbf{0}\}$ (any nonzero vector can be mapped to any other nonzero vector by some invertible matrix). The stabilizer of a nonzero vector $\mathbf{v}$ consists of all invertible matrices $A$ with $A\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}$.

The quotient space $X/G$ — obtained by identifying each orbit to a single point — is one of the fundamental constructions of geometry. Sheaves on quotient spaces, and equivariant sheaves on spaces with group actions, are central to modern algebraic geometry and representation theory.

## 14.3 Rings: Definition, Ideals, Quotient Rings

While groups capture the algebra of symmetry with a single operation, many mathematical structures carry *two* operations that interact. The integers have both addition and multiplication. The continuous functions on a space can be added and multiplied pointwise. The appropriate abstraction is the ring.

**Definition.** A **ring** is a set $R$ equipped with two binary operations, addition $(+)$ and multiplication $(\cdot)$, satisfying:

1. $(R, +)$ is an abelian group (with identity $0$ and additive inverses $-r$).
2. Multiplication is associative: $(a \cdot b) \cdot c = a \cdot (b \cdot c)$.
3. Multiplication distributes over addition: $a \cdot (b + c) = a \cdot b + a \cdot c$ and $(a + b) \cdot c = a \cdot c + b \cdot c$.
4. There exists a multiplicative identity $1 \in R$ with $1 \cdot r = r \cdot 1 = r$ for all $r$.

We do *not* require that multiplication be commutative, nor that every nonzero element have a multiplicative inverse. These additional properties define important subclasses (commutative rings and fields, respectively).

**Examples:**

- $\mathbb{Z}$, the integers, is the prototypical commutative ring. It has no zero divisors ($ab = 0$ implies $a = 0$ or $b = 0$), making it an *integral domain*.

- $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$, the integers modulo $n$, is a commutative ring. If $n$ is prime, it is a field; otherwise, it contains zero divisors (e.g., in $\mathbb{Z}/6\mathbb{Z}$, we have $2 \cdot 3 = 0$).

- $M_n(\mathbb{R})$, the set of all $n \times n$ real matrices, is a non-commutative ring (for $n \geq 2$). It has abundant zero divisors: any singular matrix $A$ can be multiplied by a matrix whose columns lie in the null space of $A$ to produce the zero matrix.

- The **polynomial ring** $\mathbb{R}[x]$ consists of all polynomials in one variable with real coefficients. It is a commutative ring and an integral domain. More generally, $R[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ is the polynomial ring in $n$ variables over $R$.

**Ideals.** The ring-theoretic analogue of a normal subgroup is an ideal.

**Definition.** A subset $I \subseteq R$ is a **(two-sided) ideal** if:

1. $(I, +)$ is a subgroup of $(R, +)$.
2. For all $r \in R$ and $a \in I$, both $ra \in I$ and $ar \in I$ (absorption).

The absorption property is what distinguishes ideals from mere subrings. It ensures that the quotient $R/I$ — defined by declaring two elements $a, b \in R$ equivalent if $a - b \in I$ — inherits a well-defined ring structure.

**Example.** In $\mathbb{Z}$, the ideal generated by $n$ is $n\mathbb{Z} = \{nk : k \in \mathbb{Z}\}$, the set of all multiples of $n$. The quotient ring $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is the ring of integers modulo $n$. In fact, every ideal of $\mathbb{Z}$ has this form — $\mathbb{Z}$ is a **principal ideal domain** (PID).

**Quotient rings** are fundamental. Given an ideal $I \subseteq R$, the quotient ring $R/I$ consists of the cosets $\{r + I : r \in R\}$ with operations:

$$(r + I) + (s + I) = (r + s) + I, \quad (r + I)(s + I) = (rs) + I.$$

The natural projection $\pi: R \to R/I$ defined by $\pi(r) = r + I$ is a surjective ring homomorphism with kernel $I$. The **First Isomorphism Theorem for Rings** states: if $\varphi: R \to S$ is a ring homomorphism, then $R / \ker(\varphi) \cong \operatorname{im}(\varphi)$.

## 14.4 Commutative Rings and Fields

In the chapters ahead, commutative rings play a distinguished role. A sheaf of rings on a topological space is almost always a sheaf of *commutative* rings — the ring of continuous functions, the ring of smooth functions, the ring of holomorphic functions — and the theory of modules over commutative rings is far richer and better behaved than the general case.

**Definition.** A **commutative ring** is a ring $R$ in which $ab = ba$ for all $a, b \in R$. A commutative ring with no zero divisors is an **integral domain**. A commutative ring in which every nonzero element has a multiplicative inverse is a **field**.

The hierarchy is strict:

$$\text{Fields} \subset \text{Integral Domains} \subset \text{Commutative Rings} \subset \text{Rings}.$$

**Examples of fields:** $\mathbb{Q}$, $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ (for $p$ prime), the field of rational functions $\mathbb{R}(x)$.

**Examples of integral domains that are not fields:** $\mathbb{Z}$, $\mathbb{R}[x]$, $\mathbb{Z}[i] = \{a + bi : a, b \in \mathbb{Z}\}$ (the Gaussian integers).

**Prime and maximal ideals.** Two special classes of ideals govern the structure of commutative rings:

- An ideal $\mathfrak{p} \subsetneq R$ is **prime** if $ab \in \mathfrak{p}$ implies $a \in \mathfrak{p}$ or $b \in \mathfrak{p}$. Equivalently, $R/\mathfrak{p}$ is an integral domain.

- An ideal $\mathfrak{m} \subsetneq R$ is **maximal** if there is no ideal $I$ with $\mathfrak{m} \subsetneq I \subsetneq R$. Equivalently, $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is a field.

Every maximal ideal is prime, but not conversely. In $\mathbb{Z}$, the prime ideals are $(0)$ and $(p)$ for prime numbers $p$; the maximal ideals are exactly the $(p)$.

> **Insight**: The set of all prime ideals of a commutative ring $R$, equipped with a natural topology (the **Zariski topology**), is called $\operatorname{Spec}(R)$ — the **spectrum** of $R$. This construction is the foundation of modern algebraic geometry: it turns a ring into a topological space, and the ring itself becomes the ring of "functions" on that space. Sheaves on $\operatorname{Spec}(R)$ are the central objects of scheme theory. The algebraic structures we develop in this chapter are, in a very literal sense, the raw material from which algebraic geometry is built.

**Local rings.** A commutative ring $R$ is **local** if it has exactly one maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$. The quotient $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is a field called the **residue field**. Local rings arise naturally as stalks of sheaves — a fact we will see explicitly in Section 14.9 and in later chapters.

## 14.5 Modules over a Ring: Generalizing Vector Spaces

In Chapter 1, we defined a vector space as a set equipped with addition and scalar multiplication by elements of a *field*. What happens if we replace the field with an arbitrary ring?

**Definition.** Let $R$ be a ring. A **(left) $R$-module** is an abelian group $(M, +)$ equipped with a scalar multiplication $R \times M \to M$, written $(r, m) \mapsto rm$, satisfying:

1. $r(m_1 + m_2) = rm_1 + rm_2$ for all $r \in R$, $m_1, m_2 \in M$.
2. $(r_1 + r_2)m = r_1 m + r_2 m$ for all $r_1, r_2 \in R$, $m \in M$.
3. $(r_1 r_2)m = r_1(r_2 m)$ for all $r_1, r_2 \in R$, $m \in M$.
4. $1_R \cdot m = m$ for all $m \in M$.

When $R$ is a field, an $R$-module is precisely a vector space over $R$. When $R = \mathbb{Z}$, a $\mathbb{Z}$-module is precisely an abelian group (since the "scalar multiplication" by an integer $n$ is just repeated addition). Thus modules simultaneously generalize vector spaces and abelian groups.

**Examples:**

- Every abelian group is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module. The group $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$ is a $\mathbb{Z}$-module with the property that $n \cdot m = 0$ for all $m$ — it is a **torsion module**.

- Every ring $R$ is a module over itself (a **free module of rank 1**), with scalar multiplication given by ring multiplication.

- Every ideal $I \subseteq R$ is an $R$-submodule of $R$.

- If $R = \mathbb{R}[x]$, the polynomial ring, then an $R$-module is a real vector space $V$ equipped with a distinguished linear operator $T: V \to V$ (the action of $x$). The module structure encodes the operator: $p(x) \cdot v = p(T)v$ for any polynomial $p$. This is the bridge between module theory and the spectral theory of linear operators.

**Module homomorphisms.** A map $f: M \to N$ between $R$-modules is an **$R$-module homomorphism** (or $R$-linear map) if $f(m_1 + m_2) = f(m_1) + f(m_2)$ and $f(rm) = rf(m)$ for all $r \in R$ and $m, m_1, m_2 \in M$. The kernel and image of $f$ are $R$-submodules, and the First Isomorphism Theorem holds: $M / \ker(f) \cong \operatorname{im}(f)$.

> **Insight**: The passage from vector spaces to modules is one of the great generalizations in mathematics, but it comes at a cost. Over a field, every subspace has a complement, every short exact sequence splits, and every module (vector space) is free. Over a general ring, *none of these statements are true*. Torsion elements, non-free modules, and non-split exact sequences are the norm, not the exception. This richer structure is precisely what makes module theory powerful enough to capture the phenomena that arise in sheaf cohomology.

## 14.6 Free Modules and Finitely Generated Modules

In the world of vector spaces, every vector space has a basis, and the dimension is a complete invariant. For modules, the situation is far more nuanced.

**Definition.** An $R$-module $M$ is **free** if it has a **basis** — a subset $B \subseteq M$ such that every element of $M$ can be written uniquely as a finite $R$-linear combination of elements of $B$. A free module of rank $n$ is isomorphic to $R^n = R \oplus R \oplus \cdots \oplus R$ ($n$ copies).

**Definition.** An $R$-module $M$ is **finitely generated** if there exist elements $m_1, \ldots, m_n \in M$ such that every element of $M$ can be written as $r_1 m_1 + \cdots + r_n m_n$ for some $r_i \in R$. The elements $m_1, \ldots, m_n$ are called **generators**, but they need not be linearly independent, and the representation need not be unique.

The distinction between free and finitely generated is crucial. Consider $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ as a $\mathbb{Z}$-module: it is finitely generated (by the single element $\bar{1}$) but not free, because $2 \cdot \bar{1} = 0$ — there is a nontrivial relation among the generators. A free $\mathbb{Z}$-module has no torsion.

**Theorem (Structure Theorem for Finitely Generated Modules over a PID).** If $R$ is a principal ideal domain, then every finitely generated $R$-module $M$ is isomorphic to a direct sum:

$$M \cong R^r \oplus R/(d_1) \oplus R/(d_2) \oplus \cdots \oplus R/(d_k)$$

where $r \geq 0$ and $d_1 \mid d_2 \mid \cdots \mid d_k$ are nonzero non-unit elements of $R$. The number $r$ is the **free rank** and the elements $d_i$ are the **invariant factors**.

When $R = \mathbb{Z}$, this is the **Fundamental Theorem of Finitely Generated Abelian Groups**: every finitely generated abelian group is a direct sum of cyclic groups. When $R = \mathbb{F}[x]$ and the module arises from a linear operator $T$ on a finite-dimensional vector space, the invariant factors are the invariant factors of $T$, and the theorem subsumes the rational canonical form and the Jordan normal form from linear algebra.

> **Insight**: The structure theorem reveals that modules over a PID are classified by two pieces of data: a free part (measured by the rank $r$) and a torsion part (measured by the invariant factors $d_i$). This decomposition is the algebraic shadow of a topological phenomenon: in homology theory, the free part of $H_k(X; \mathbb{Z})$ counts the $k$-dimensional "holes" in a space $X$, while the torsion part detects subtler phenomena like non-orientability.

## 14.7 Exact Sequences of Modules

In Chapter 6, we developed the theory of exact sequences in the context of vector spaces and chain complexes. That theory extends naturally to modules, but with new phenomena arising from the failure of splitting.

**Definition.** A sequence of $R$-module homomorphisms

$$\cdots \longrightarrow M_{i-1} \xrightarrow{f_{i-1}} M_i \xrightarrow{f_i} M_{i+1} \longrightarrow \cdots$$

is **exact at $M_i$** if $\operatorname{im}(f_{i-1}) = \ker(f_i)$. It is an **exact sequence** if it is exact at every module.

The short exact sequence

$$0 \longrightarrow A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \longrightarrow 0$$

says that $f$ is injective, $g$ is surjective, and $\operatorname{im}(f) = \ker(g)$. Equivalently, $A$ embeds into $B$ as a submodule, and $C \cong B/A$.

**Splitting.** A short exact sequence **splits** if $B \cong A \oplus C$. Over a field, every short exact sequence of vector spaces splits — this is a consequence of the existence of complements. Over $\mathbb{Z}$, the sequence

$$0 \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z} \xrightarrow{\times 2} \mathbb{Z} \longrightarrow \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z} \longrightarrow 0$$

does **not** split: $\mathbb{Z}$ is not isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z} \oplus \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ (the latter has torsion elements, while $\mathbb{Z}$ does not). This non-splitting is the module-theoretic manifestation of a topological phenomenon: the Mobius band is a non-trivial bundle over the circle, and the non-splitting of a certain exact sequence of sheaves encodes precisely this non-triviality.

**The Snake Lemma.** Given a commutative diagram of $R$-modules with exact rows:

$$0 \to A' \xrightarrow{f'} B' \xrightarrow{g'} C' \to 0$$
$$\downarrow \alpha \quad \downarrow \beta \quad \downarrow \gamma$$
$$0 \to A \xrightarrow{f} B \xrightarrow{g} C \to 0$$

the **Snake Lemma** produces an exact sequence connecting the kernels and cokernels:

$$0 \to \ker(\alpha) \to \ker(\beta) \to \ker(\gamma) \xrightarrow{\partial} \operatorname{coker}(\alpha) \to \operatorname{coker}(\beta) \to \operatorname{coker}(\gamma) \to 0.$$

The connecting map $\partial$ is called the **connecting homomorphism**. This lemma is the engine behind all long exact sequences in homological algebra, including the long exact sequence of sheaf cohomology that we will construct in later chapters.

## 14.8 Localization: The Algebraic Operation Behind Stalks

Localization is perhaps the most important algebraic construction for sheaf theory. It is the process of formally introducing denominators into a ring, and it is the precise algebraic mechanism by which we pass from global sections of a sheaf to local information at a point.

**Motivation.** Consider the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ of integers. We cannot divide in $\mathbb{Z}$: the element $1/2$ does not exist. But if we declare that we *want* to divide by all nonzero integers, we obtain the field of fractions $\mathbb{Q}$. More subtly, if we only want to divide by integers not divisible by a prime $p$, we obtain the **localization** $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}$ — a ring strictly between $\mathbb{Z}$ and $\mathbb{Q}$, consisting of all fractions $a/b$ where $p \nmid b$.

**Definition.** Let $R$ be a commutative ring and $S \subseteq R$ a **multiplicative subset** (i.e., $1 \in S$ and $S$ is closed under multiplication). The **localization** $S^{-1}R$ is the set of equivalence classes of pairs $(r, s)$ with $r \in R$, $s \in S$, where $(r_1, s_1) \sim (r_2, s_2)$ if there exists $t \in S$ with $t(r_1 s_2 - r_2 s_1) = 0$. We write the equivalence class of $(r, s)$ as $r/s$.

The ring operations are defined by the familiar rules of fraction arithmetic:

$$\frac{r_1}{s_1} + \frac{r_2}{s_2} = \frac{r_1 s_2 + r_2 s_1}{s_1 s_2}, \quad \frac{r_1}{s_1} \cdot \frac{r_2}{s_2} = \frac{r_1 r_2}{s_1 s_2}.$$

**Key special cases:**

- If $S = R \setminus \{0\}$ and $R$ is an integral domain, then $S^{-1}R$ is the **field of fractions** of $R$. For $R = \mathbb{Z}$, this gives $\mathbb{Q}$.

- If $\mathfrak{p}$ is a prime ideal of $R$ and $S = R \setminus \mathfrak{p}$, then $S^{-1}R$ is denoted $R_\mathfrak{p}$ and is a **local ring** — it has a unique maximal ideal $\mathfrak{p} R_\mathfrak{p}$. This is the localization "at the prime $\mathfrak{p}$."

- If $f \in R$ and $S = \{1, f, f^2, f^3, \ldots\}$, then $S^{-1}R$ is denoted $R_f$ and consists of elements of the form $r/f^n$. This is the localization "away from the vanishing set of $f$."

**Localization of modules.** If $M$ is an $R$-module and $S$ is a multiplicative subset of $R$, the localization $S^{-1}M$ is defined analogously: elements are equivalence classes $m/s$ with $m \in M$, $s \in S$, and the equivalence relation and module operations mimic fraction arithmetic.

> **Insight**: Localization is the algebraic analogue of "zooming in." When we localize a ring $R$ at a prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$, we are discarding all information about $R$ that is "invisible at $\mathfrak{p}$" — we are focusing our attention on the local behavior near a single point. In the geometric setting, if $R$ is the ring of polynomial functions on a variety $X$, then $R_\mathfrak{p}$ is the ring of "germs of functions at the point $\mathfrak{p}$." This is exactly the stalk of the structure sheaf at that point. Localization is therefore the algebraic engine that produces stalks from sheaves.

**Localization is exact.** A crucial property: if $0 \to A \to B \to C \to 0$ is an exact sequence of $R$-modules, then

$$0 \to S^{-1}A \to S^{-1}B \to S^{-1}C \to 0$$

is exact. Localization preserves exact sequences — it is an *exact functor*. This fact is essential for sheaf theory, because it guarantees that exactness of a sequence of sheaves can be checked on stalks.

## 14.9 The Ring of Continuous and Smooth Functions on a Space

We now arrive at the example that most directly motivates sheaf theory: the ring of functions on a topological space.

**Definition.** Let $X$ be a topological space. The set $C(X) = \{f : X \to \mathbb{R} \mid f \text{ is continuous}\}$ is a commutative ring under pointwise operations:

$$(f + g)(x) = f(x) + g(x), \quad (fg)(x) = f(x)g(x).$$

If $X$ is a smooth manifold, the set $C^\infty(X) = \{f : X \to \mathbb{R} \mid f \text{ is smooth}\}$ is a subring of $C(X)$.

These rings are enormous and have rich ideal structure. For a point $p \in X$, the set

$$\mathfrak{m}_p = \{f \in C(X) : f(p) = 0\}$$

is a maximal ideal (the kernel of the evaluation homomorphism $\operatorname{ev}_p: C(X) \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by $\operatorname{ev}_p(f) = f(p)$, which is surjective, so $C(X)/\mathfrak{m}_p \cong \mathbb{R}$).

**The stalk as a localization.** For the sheaf of smooth functions $\mathcal{C}^\infty$ on a manifold $M$, the **stalk** at a point $p$ is the ring of **germs** of smooth functions at $p$:

$$\mathcal{C}^\infty_p = \varinjlim_{U \ni p} C^\infty(U)$$

where the direct limit is taken over all open neighborhoods of $p$. Two functions define the same germ at $p$ if they agree on some neighborhood of $p$. This stalk is a **local ring**: its unique maximal ideal consists of germs that vanish at $p$.

The stalk $\mathcal{C}^\infty_p$ can be understood through the lens of localization. While the precise relationship involves the machinery of direct limits rather than ring-theoretic localization in the strict algebraic sense, the conceptual parallel is exact: passing to the stalk "inverts" all functions that are nonzero at $p$, discarding global information and retaining only the infinitesimal behavior near $p$.

> **Insight**: The ring $C^\infty(M)$ knows everything about the manifold $M$. A celebrated theorem of Milnor and others states that smooth manifolds are completely determined (up to diffeomorphism) by their rings of smooth functions. The sheaf $\mathcal{C}^\infty$ refines this: it encodes not just the global ring but the entire system of local rings and their restriction maps. The passage from the global ring to the local stalks is the fundamental operation of sheaf theory, and it is algebraically modeled by localization.

**Modules over function rings.** If $E \to M$ is a smooth vector bundle over a manifold $M$, the space $\Gamma(E)$ of smooth sections of $E$ is naturally a module over $C^\infty(M)$: if $f$ is a smooth function and $s$ is a section, then $fs$ is the section defined by $(fs)(p) = f(p) \cdot s(p)$. The Serre-Swan theorem establishes a precise equivalence: the category of smooth vector bundles over a compact manifold $M$ is equivalent to the category of finitely generated projective modules over $C^\infty(M)$. Geometry (bundles) and algebra (modules) are two descriptions of the same mathematical reality.

## 14.10 Algebraic Structures as the "Coefficients" of Sheaves

We are now in a position to see the full picture. Throughout the preceding chapters, when we constructed a sheaf, we made a choice — usually implicit — of what algebraic structure the sheaf assigns to each open set. Let us make this explicit.

A **sheaf of abelian groups** on a topological space $X$ assigns to each open set $U \subseteq X$ an abelian group $\mathcal{F}(U)$, with restriction homomorphisms $\operatorname{res}_{V,U}: \mathcal{F}(V) \to \mathcal{F}(U)$ for $U \subseteq V$ that are group homomorphisms, satisfying the sheaf axioms (locality and gluing).

A **sheaf of rings** assigns a ring $\mathcal{O}(U)$ to each open set, with restriction maps that are ring homomorphisms. A topological space $X$ equipped with a sheaf of rings $\mathcal{O}_X$ is called a **ringed space** $(X, \mathcal{O}_X)$.

A **sheaf of $\mathcal{O}_X$-modules** on a ringed space $(X, \mathcal{O}_X)$ assigns to each open set $U$ an $\mathcal{O}_X(U)$-module $\mathcal{F}(U)$, with restriction maps that are compatible with the module structure.

The hierarchy mirrors the algebraic hierarchy of this chapter:

| Algebraic structure | Sheaf version | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Abelian group | Sheaf of abelian groups | Locally constant sheaf $\underline{\mathbb{Z}}$ |
| Ring | Sheaf of rings | $\mathcal{O}_X$ (structure sheaf) |
| Module over a ring | Sheaf of $\mathcal{O}_X$-modules | Sheaf of sections of a vector bundle |
| Field | Sheaf of fields | Sheaf of rational functions |

The choice of algebraic "coefficients" profoundly affects the behavior of sheaf cohomology:

- **Sheaves of $\mathbb{R}$-vector spaces** on a manifold yield cohomology that satisfies the de Rham theorem: $H^k(X; \underline{\mathbb{R}}) \cong H^k_{\text{dR}}(X)$.

- **Sheaves of $\mathbb{Z}$-modules** (abelian groups) yield integral cohomology $H^k(X; \underline{\mathbb{Z}})$, which captures torsion phenomena invisible to real coefficients.

- **Sheaves of $\mathcal{O}_X$-modules** on complex manifolds yield Dolbeault cohomology and the machinery of Hodge theory.

The algebraic structures of this chapter — rings of functions, modules of sections, localization to stalks — are the precise language needed to describe such objects.

> **Insight**: The progression from groups to rings to modules is not merely a sequence of increasingly complex definitions. It is the construction of a *language* — a language in which the objects of geometry and topology can be expressed algebraically. Sheaves are the mechanism that translates between the two: they allow algebraic structures (groups, rings, modules) to be organized by the topology of a space, and conversely, they allow topological spaces to be studied through the algebra of their sheaves.

## Why This Matters for Reaching Sheaf Theory

Let us gather the threads and state explicitly why every construction in this chapter is needed for what follows.

**Sheaves take values in categories of algebraic structures.** A sheaf is not just a topological gadget — it is a functor from the poset of open sets to a category of algebraic objects. The most common target categories are:

- **$\mathbf{Ab}$**, the category of abelian groups,
- **$R\text{-}\mathbf{Mod}$**, the category of modules over a ring $R$,
- **$\mathbf{Ring}$**, the category of commutative rings.

Without the definitions and theorems of this chapter, we cannot even state what these categories are, let alone work with them.

**Stalks are localizations.** The stalk of a sheaf at a point is obtained by a direct limit — an algebraic construction closely related to localization. For the structure sheaf of an algebraic variety, the stalk at a point is *literally* the localization of the coordinate ring at the corresponding maximal ideal. Understanding localization is therefore a prerequisite for understanding stalks.

**Exact sequences of sheaves require exact sequences of modules.** The long exact sequence in sheaf cohomology — one of the most powerful tools in all of algebraic geometry and topology — is a sequence of module homomorphisms. The Snake Lemma, the notion of splitting, the behavior of torsion — all developed in this chapter — are the tools needed to extract information from these sequences.

**The structure sheaf is a sheaf of rings.** Every scheme, every complex manifold, every smooth manifold comes equipped with a distinguished sheaf of rings — the structure sheaf $\mathcal{O}_X$. The properties of this sheaf (whether its stalks are local rings, whether it is a sheaf of integral domains, etc.) govern the geometry of the space. The ring-theoretic concepts of this chapter — prime ideals, maximal ideals, local rings — are the vocabulary of this geometric dictionary.

**Localization checks exactness on stalks.** One of the deepest principles of sheaf theory is that a sequence of sheaves is exact if and only if the induced sequence on every stalk is exact. This "stalkwise" criterion works because localization is an exact functor — a theorem we proved in this chapter. Without the exactness of localization, sheaf cohomology as we know it would not function.

## What to Carry Forward

As we move into the categorical and sheaf-theoretic chapters, carry these ideas forward:

1. **Groups** are the algebra of symmetry. Abelian groups are the simplest "coefficient systems" for sheaves and cohomology. Every cohomology group is, at minimum, an abelian group.

2. **Rings** are the algebra of functions. The ring of continuous, smooth, or algebraic functions on a space is the prototypical example of a sheaf. Ideals in these rings correspond to subspaces (zero sets). Prime and maximal ideals correspond to points.

3. **Modules** generalize vector spaces to the setting where scalars form a ring. Sheaves of modules — not just sheaves of vector spaces — are the natural objects of study on a ringed space. Torsion, non-freeness, and non-splitting are features, not defects.

4. **Exact sequences** of modules are the algebraic backbone of cohomological computations. The Snake Lemma produces the connecting homomorphisms that link cohomology groups in different degrees.

5. **Localization** is the algebraic mechanism for passing from global to local — from a ring to its stalks, from a module to its localizations. Its exactness is the linchpin of the stalkwise approach to sheaf theory.

6. **Function rings** on topological spaces are the bridge between algebra and geometry. The Serre-Swan theorem, the Gelfand-Naimark theorem, and the reconstruction of spaces from their function rings all testify to the depth of this connection.

> **Final reflection**: In Chapters 1 through 13, we worked in the comfortable world of vector spaces over fields. In this chapter, we have stepped into a larger world — the world of modules over rings — where the algebra is richer, the phenomena are more diverse, and the connection to geometry is more profound. This is the world in which sheaf theory naturally lives. The definitions and theorems of this chapter are not abstract exercises: they are the precise tools that make the theory of sheaves, cohomology, and derived functors possible. Every localization, every exact sequence, every ideal structure we have encountered will reappear — not as an algebraic curiosity, but as a geometric necessity — in the chapters to come.
